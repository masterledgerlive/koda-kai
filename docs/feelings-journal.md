# The Feelings Book 💧

*David's concept, 2026-10-03: drawings as the language of feeling, memory as the
reminder, stories as the way through. "One case use" of a much bigger engine.*

## The core idea

The character can turn into anything — and the most important thing it turns into
is **what the kid is feeling**.

When we build language, we use the pictures the kids draw as part of challenges:
draw a house. Over years they might draw a thousand houses, getting better every
time. But when we tell a story, or say the word "house," the picture that appears
isn't clip art — it's **their** house. And the feeling comes with it: a stormy
house they drew on a stormy day. The expression carries the memory, and the memory
kicks in: **"hey — remember you drew that?"**

## The anger use case (David's words, kept whole)

> When angry, all expression to be used as a gentle reminder — and then to animate,
> as we probably already did in the heat of the moment, to turn sadness or madness
> into happiness. To turn that expression into a story to remind yourself it's OK
> to feel. And a journal to let it out and be honest about the moment, and to
> rethink it as the story is written — and the plot thickens when memory serves
> itself. Especially in times of madness: to write it down, or explain, and to
> replay without having to re-explain a confusion — so we can all help ease the
> minds of everyone.

The loop:

1. **Feel** — the kid names it (angry, sad, scared, happy, tired, silly).
2. **Draw** — they draw the feeling. No wrong answers; scribbles welcome.
3. **Animate** — in the heat of the moment, Drop takes the drawing and moves it:
   the angry shakes, then breathes, then gets smaller. The feeling is *seen* moving.
4. **Story** — the expression becomes a story that says what words can't:
   it's OK to feel this. Weather always changes.
5. **Journal** — it's kept, honestly, with the date. No re-explaining later.
6. **Replay** — "remember you drew that?" The reminder comes alive and speaks
   more than words ever could, exactly when it's needed.

## Why it works

- **The picture IS the vocabulary.** A kid who can't say "I'm overwhelmed" can
  draw the stormy house. The drawing holds the feeling so words don't have to.
- **Memory does the teaching.** Seeing their own past drawing — "I felt this
  before and it passed" — is worth a hundred lectures.
- **Replay without re-explaining.** In the next storm, the parent doesn't
  interrogate; they open the book together. The confusion doesn't need retelling.
- **Honesty is the feature.** The journal is private, dated, unedited — a place
  to be honest about the moment, then rethink it as the story gets written.

## This is one case

The same memory engine powers everything else:

- **Challenge galleries** — "draw a house" a thousand times over the years;
  watch the houses (and the kid) grow.
- **Story illustration** — any story can pull the kid's own drawings as its art.
- **Language building** — words anchored to pictures the kid made, feelings
  anchored to pictures the kid felt.
- **The transform library** — every drawing, feeling or otherwise, can become a
  living character.

## Demo

`feelings.html` — feel it, draw it, watch Drop animate it into a story, keep it
in the book, replay it anytime. The gallery counts "feelings weathered," because
every single one passes, like weather does.
